1 Counting Frames

Watch the big number for one second and it goes up by 60.

Back in Getting Started we put hello's drawing inside function _draw() and never asked why. Picotron calls that function over and over while a program runs, and it calls one named _update just before each one. Those two calls going round until you press Escape are the game loop.

The line above the functions runs once, when the program starts. It makes a variable called frames, a name that holds a value for later, and puts 0 in it.

Then the loop takes over. Our update adds 1 to frames, since += means add this onto whatever's there already. Our draw clears the screen to dark blue and prints frames twice as wide and twice as tall, which is what the \^w\^t in front of it does, and the two dots join the number onto that.

Under it we print t(), the seconds since the program started. Picotron doesn't read that off a clock, it counts updates, so it goes up a 60th of a second every time frames goes up by 1.

Why two functions, when we could do the adding at the top of the draw? You could, and it works right up until there's too much to draw.

Picotron calls _update 60 times a second. When drawing can't keep up it skips draws instead and drops to 30 or 20 draws a second. Each draw then gets two or three updates to make up the difference, so anything we move in the update keeps its speed. Below 20 it stops making up the difference and the whole game slows down.

So the frames counter here is really counting updates, and it only matches the frames drawn while Picotron keeps up.

If you know PICO-8, Picotron has no _update60. Our _update already runs at 60, and a function named _update60 just never gets called.

The whole demo:

demos/1_frames.lua
1-- count the updates, and show the clock
2frames = 0
3
4function _update()
5  frames += 1
6end
7
8function _draw()
9  cls(1)
10  -- \^w\^t prints twice as wide and tall
11  print("\^w\^t"..frames, 200, 100, 7)
12  print("frames", 200, 130, 6)
13  print("t() = "..t(), 200, 160, 12)
14end

Try This

  • Turn it into a countdown from 10. The first thing I ever made, on a Tandy Color Computer 2 from a BASIC listing in a paper manual, was pretty much this, with a rocket drawn in text characters.

2 Wiping the Screen

Turn clear off and the ball smears a fat blue snake across the screen.

Picotron never clears the screen for us. Whatever we drew last frame is still there when the next draw starts, and the cls that was wiping the old ball only runs while clear is 1.

clear starts at 1, so by default the screen gets wiped. x gets 40 and y gets 60, which is where the ball starts. dx and dy are how far it moves every update, and here it moves 3 pixels right and 2 down each time. Putting commas on both sides of the equals sign sets two variables in one line.

Every update we add dx to x and dy to y. The first if asks whether x is under 16 or over 463, which is true when the ball has gone past the left edge or the right one. 16 is the ball's radius and 463 is the last column, 479, minus 16, so the test is about the ball's edge touching the screen's edge.

When it's true, dx = -dx flips the sign. Our 3 becomes -3 and the ball heads back the other way. The second if does the same for y, with 253 at the bottom.

The ball is one ball in two parts. circfill draws the light blue middle and circ puts the white outline round it.

Leave clear at 0 and you've pretty much got a paint program. A game nearly always starts its draw with cls.

The whole demo:

demos/2_trail.lua
1-- a bouncing ball: is the screen wiped?
2clear = 1
3x, y = 40, 60
4dx, dy = 3, 2
5
6function _update()
7  x += dx
8  y += dy
9  if x < 16 or x > 463 then dx = -dx end
10  if y < 16 or y > 253 then dy = -dy end
11end
12
13function _draw()
14  if clear == 1 then cls(1) end
15  circfill(x, y, 16, 12)
16  circ(x, y, 16, 7)
17end

3 Pixels Per Second

Drag pps and the ball jumps.

We never add anything to x here. Every update works it out from scratch as t() * pps, which is how far a ball going pps pixels a second would have got by now, so a new speed puts the ball wherever it would be if it had always gone that fast. Then % 480 keeps what's left after taking out every whole 480. At 500 we get 20, and the ball comes back on the left.

Pixels per second are easy to think in. The ball on the last slide moved 3 pixels an update, and 60 updates a second makes that 180 pixels a second. It crossed the screen in two and two-thirds seconds. At the 60 pps we start with here, it's exactly 1 pixel an update.

Working out x from the time is good for a cloud drifting past. It's no use for anything that bounces or that you steer, since nothing remembers where the ball was a moment ago. For those we keep our own x and add to it, like the ball on the last slide.

The whole demo:

demos/3_steady.lua
1-- t() counts seconds; pps is pixels per second
2pps = 60
3x = 0
4
5function _update()
6  x = t() * pps % 480
7end
8
9function _draw()
10  cls(1)
11  circfill(x, 135, 12, 10)
12  print("t() = "..t(), 8, 8, 6)
13  print("x = "..x, 8, 20, 6)
14end

Try This

  • Make the ball cross the screen exactly once every 5 seconds.
  • Make it go right to left.
  • Add a second ball underneath that keeps pace with this one by adding to its own x every update.

4 Starting with _init

Leave this slide and come back, or press Ctrl + R again in Picotron, and the ball turns up somewhere else, probably in a different colour. _init picked both. It runs once each time the program starts. The lines at the top run before it and the first update comes after it.

It adds 1 to inits, then rnd picks the colour out of the list in curly brackets, which is red, orange, yellow, green, blue or pink. Give rnd a number instead and we get a random number from 0 up to just under it. It has decimals too, like 265.08476976305. So x is 40 plus up to 400 and lands somewhere from 40 to 440. y lands from 60 to 240 the same way, which keeps the ball clear of the edges.

After that it's just the loop. Each update adds 1 to updates. Each draw clears the screen, draws the ball and prints both counts, and we watch updates climb by 60 a second while inits stays at 1.

Here we could move the colour and position lines up to the top and nothing would change. It matters when the set-up uses a function we wrote further down the file, because the top runs while Picotron is still reading it and that function doesn't exist yet. Our _init waits until the whole file is read. It's an ordinary function too, so a game can call _init() again to start over.

The whole demo:

demos/4_init.lua
1-- _init runs once, before the first update
2inits = 0
3updates = 0
4
5function _init()
6  inits += 1
7  col = rnd({8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14})
8  x = 40 + rnd(400)
9  y = 60 + rnd(180)
10end
11
12function _update()
13  updates += 1
14end
15
16function _draw()
17  cls(1)
18  circfill(x, y, 20, col)
19  print("_init ran "..inits.." time", 8, 8, 7)
20  print("_update ran "..updates.." times", 8, 20, 7)
21end

Try This

  • Give the ball a random size between 10 and 40 as well.
  • Make the ball jump somewhere new on every update, without copying any lines out of _init. The inits count should climb with it.

5 Back to Front

Click + on step to take it from 1 up to 3 and the picture builds from the back. We get the sun, then a hill over the bottom of the sun, then a house on the hill, because each thing we draw lands on top of whatever's already there. People call this the painter's algorithm. The sky goes first, for the same reason a painter does it first.

There's no _update this time because nothing moves. Leave it out and _draw still runs 60 times a second. Each part sits inside an if that checks step, with >= meaning at least. The hill is a circle of radius 300 with its middle at y 470, way below the bottom of the 270-pixel screen. Only its top pokes up as far as y 170. Picotron just skips the part that's off screen. The house is all rectfill, which takes the top-left corner, the bottom-right corner and a colour. We draw the walls first, then the roof along the top of them, then the door and the window on the walls.

If something you drew vanishes, check what you drew after it.

The whole demo:

demos/5_painter.lua
1-- shapes drawn later land on top
2step = 3
3
4function _draw()
5  cls(12)
6  -- 1: the sun
7  if step >= 1 then
8    circfill(360, 200, 40, 10)
9  end
10  -- 2: a hill, in front of the sun
11  if step >= 2 then
12    circfill(220, 470, 300, 3)
13  end
14  -- 3: a house, on top of the hill
15  if step >= 3 then
16    rectfill(170, 120, 270, 190, 4) -- walls
17    rectfill(160, 100, 280, 120, 2) -- roof
18    rectfill(205, 150, 235, 190, 5) -- door
19    rectfill(180, 135, 195, 150, 7) -- window
20  end
21end

Try This

  • Give the house a chimney that sits behind the roof.
  • Make the sun set behind the hill.

6 Over to You

This is the empty one. All three functions are there, and the ball sits in the middle because nothing in our _update moves it. Start new things from this.

None of this listens to you yet, the ball goes wherever the numbers send it. Basics 4 gets us the arrow keys, but Basics 3 comes first, because we've been throwing numbers around without really looking at them.

The whole demo:

demos/6_over_to_you.lua
1function _init()
2  x, y = 240, 135
3  col = 12
4end
5
6function _update()
7  -- your ideas go here!
8end
9
10function _draw()
11  cls(1)
12  circfill(x, y, 16, col)
13end
