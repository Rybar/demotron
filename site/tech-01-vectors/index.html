Vectors and diagonal movement

Direction and distance as a pair of numbers, why diagonals are fast, and aiming at the mouse.

demos/1_problem.lua
1-- hold the arrows from the middle; z goes back
2-- the ring: one second of straight movement
3speed = 2
4x, y = 240, 135
5frames = 0
6trail = {}
7
8function _update()
9  if btn(0) then x -= speed end
10  if btn(1) then x += speed end
11  if btn(2) then y -= speed end
12  if btn(3) then y += speed end
13  if btn(0) or btn(1) or btn(2) or btn(3) then
14    frames += 1
15    -- a dot every 10 frames
16    if frames % 10 == 0 then
17      add(trail, {x = x, y = y})
18    end
19  end
20  if btnp(4) then
21    x, y, frames, trail = 240, 135, 0, {}
22  end
23end
24
25function _draw()

27  circ(240, 135, speed * 60, 5)
28  for d in all(trail) do
29    circfill(d.x, d.y, 1, 6)
30  end
31  circfill(x, y, 6, 12)
32  print("frames moving: "..frames, 8, 8, 7)
33end

demos/5_aim.lua
1-- hold the mouse button to fire at the pointer
2bullet_speed = 4
3px, py = 240, 135
4aim_x, aim_y = 1, 0
5bullets = {}
6cool = 0
7
8function _update()
9  local mx, my, mb = mouse()
10  -- the vector from the player to the mouse
11  local dx, dy = mx - px, my - py
12  local len = sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy)
13  if len > 0 then
14    aim_x, aim_y = dx / len, dy / len
15  end
16  cool -= 1
17  if mb > 0 and cool <= 0 then
18    add(bullets, {x = px, y = py,
19      dx = aim_x * bullet_speed,
20      dy = aim_y * bullet_speed})
21    cool = 8
22  end
23  for b in all(bullets) do
24    b.x += b.dx
25    b.y += b.dy
26    if b.x < 0 or b.x > 479
27      or b.y < 0 or b.y > 269 then
28      del(bullets, b)
29    end
30  end
31end

1 Fast diagonals

Basics 4's mover has a bug that turns up in a lot of first games: hold two arrows and the player goes faster. In this lesson we fix it, and the fix gives us vectors, the pair of numbers behind every direction in a game. By the end a turret aims and fires at the mouse, and a chaser walks straight at the player.

This is Basics 4's four-way mover with two additions. The grey ring shows how far one second (60 frames) of straight movement goes, and the circle drops a dot every 10 frames while it moves. Z (Picotron's O button) puts it back in the middle.

Hold right: the circle reaches the ring after 60 frames, with the dots 20 pixels apart. Press Z, then hold right and down. It gets to the ring after about 42 frames, and the dots are about 28 pixels apart. Each frame it moves 2 across and 2 down, and the diagonal of a 2 by 2 square is about 2.83 long, not 2. That's 41% faster, in all four diagonal directions.

Players find this quickly and move diagonally everywhere. Doom players have a name for their version: strafe-running, where walking forward and sideways at once beats walking forward.

Try this

  • Set speed to 1. Before you try it, work out how many frames a diagonal now takes to reach the ring. Then check.
  • Some games only allow four directions (Zelda on the NES, Pokémon). Make the circle move in one direction at a time: when two arrows are held, the one pressed most recently wins. You'll need btnp and a variable that remembers the last press.

2 Vectors

The fix starts with how the buttons are read. Instead of each button moving x or y itself, the buttons build two numbers, dx and dy: how far we want to go across and down this frame. Left makes dx -1 and right +1; up and down do the same to dy. Then two lines do the moving, multiplied by speed. (mid keeps the circle on screen, as in Basics 5.)

A pair of numbers like that is a vector. It's a direction and a distance in one: (1, 0) is one step right, (0, -1) one step up (y counts down), and (1, 1) one step down and right. The yellow line is the vector, drawn 30 times longer so we can see it.

Basics 5's bouncing ball already had one: its dx, dy was its velocity, the vector it moved by every frame. Here the vector is rebuilt from the buttons every frame, and it's (0, 0) when nothing is held.

The bug is still there, and now we can see it: hold right and down, and the yellow line is longer than when you hold right alone. With the vector in two numbers of its own we can measure it, which is next.

Try this

  • Draw a second line for how far the circle actually moved since the last frame (keep the old x, y before moving). Push into a wall. When do the two lines disagree, and why?
  • Give the circle momentum, like Basics 5's ball: keep a velocity vx, vy between frames, add the buttons' dx, dy times 0.2 to it each frame, then multiply it by 0.9 for friction and move by it. Before you try it, predict whether the diagonal is still faster at top speed.

3 Length

How long is a vector? For (3, 0) it's 3, and for (0, -4) it's 4. For a diagonal one, dx and dy are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle and the vector is the long side, so Pythagoras gives its length: sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy). (sqrt is the square root.)

Move the mouse around. mouse() gives the pointer's x and y first (the rest of what it returns is on slide 5). The red line is dx, the green line is dy, and the yellow line is the vector from the middle of the screen to the pointer. The grid squares are 10 pixels: put the pointer 3 squares right and 4 down, and the length is exactly 50.

That explains slide 1. The vector (1, 1) is sqrt(2) long, about 1.414, so every frame on a diagonal the circle covered 1.414 times the ground. The same sum gives the distance between any two points: Basics 4's last challenge used it to tell when two circles touch.

PICO-8 programmers will know a different 181: in PICO-8's fixed-point numbers, dx * dx overflows once dx passes 181. Picotron's numbers are 64 bits, so lengths across the whole screen (and far beyond it) are fine.

Advanced: Picotron's own vectors

Picotron has a vector type built in. vec(3, 4) makes a small userdata of 64-bit floats, and arithmetic works on every element at once: vec(1, 2) + vec(10, 20) is (11, 22), and v * 2 doubles both. v:magnitude() is the length, a:distance(b) the distance between two points, a:dot(b) the dot product and a:cross(b) the cross product. The elements are numbered from 0 (v[0], v[1]), like all userdata, and v.x and v.y work too. The manual's "Userdata Operations" and "Matrices and Vectors" sections have the rest, including a dest argument that writes the answer into an existing userdata instead of making a new one. This lesson sticks to plain numbers so the arithmetic stays in view. Speed isn't the reason: I measured 2000 vec additions at about 8% of a frame against 3% for plain numbers, while :magnitude() was about twice as fast as the sqrt written out.

Try this

  • Put the pointer 5 squares right and 12 squares down. Predict the length before you look.
  • What's the longest vector this demo can show? Work it out from the screen's size (the pointer stays between 0, 0 and 479, 269), then check in the corners.
  • Draw a circle of radius 60 around the middle and turn the vector red while the pointer is inside it. Then do the same test without sqrt. (What can you compare dx * dx + dy * dy with instead?)

4 Normalising

Now the fix. Dividing a vector by its own length keeps its direction and makes it exactly 1 long. (1, 0) stays (1, 0), but (1, 1) becomes about (0.707, 0.707), and sqrt(0.707² + 0.707²) is 1. That's called normalising, and a vector of length 1 is a unit vector. Multiply a unit vector by speed and every direction moves exactly speed pixels a frame.

This is slide 1's demo with the highlighted lines changed. Hold right and down: the circle reaches the ring after 60 frames, the same as a straight line, and the dots are 20 pixels apart whichever way it goes.

The if len > 0 matters. With no arrow held the vector is (0, 0), its length is 0, and dividing by 0 doesn't give a position anyone wants (the last challenge below finds out what it does give). The same if does a second job: len > 0 means "an arrow is held", so the frame counter and the trail moved inside it.

Basics 4's 181 is Picotron doing this for us. btn doesn't just say yes: on a digital diagonal it reports 181 for each arrow instead of 255, and 181 / 255 is about 0.71. So reading the buttons as numbers gives a vector that's already about 1 long in every direction (1.004 on a diagonal, which is 0.4% fast):

Lua
1local dx = ((btn(1) or 0) - (btn(0) or 0)) / 255
2local dy = ((btn(3) or 0) - (btn(2) or 0)) / 255
3x += dx * speed
4y += dy * speed

So which one? Normalising works on any vector, not just the arrow keys, and the next slide aims at the mouse with it. The btn version keeps a gamepad stick's half-push: push a stick halfway and btn gives smaller numbers, the vector is half as long, and the player walks. Normalising that vector would make every push a full-speed run. I use the btn version for the player and normalising for everything else. (To keep the half-push with any vector, only divide when len > 1.)

Advanced: the rest of btn

The full form is btn(b, pl, flags). pl is the player (0, the default, is player 1). flags picks where input comes from: 0x1 controllers, 0x2 the keyboard, 0x3 both (the default). btn also answers false while a stick is inside its deadzone. Buttons 8 to 11 are a second stick (left, right, up, down), which the manual says isn't on every platform, so offer another way to do whatever it does. btn's numbers are always a position a round stick could really reach, which is why left and right can't both be held; peek(0x5400 + pl * 16 + b) reads the raw controller value instead.

Try this

  • Swap the four ifs and the normalising for the btn version above (you'll need a new way to tell when an arrow is held). Predict how many frames the diagonal takes to reach the ring now, then check.
  • Start Basics 5's bouncing ball in a random direction (rnd(2) - 1 for each of dx and dy), but always moving at exactly 3 pixels a frame.
  • Delete the if len > 0 and its end, keep the lines that were inside, and run it without touching anything. Where did the circle go? Print x, then print 0 / 0 and 1 / 0. Is that what you expected from Lua?

5 Aiming

Normalising isn't only for buttons. Any two points make a vector: from the player to the mouse it's mx - px, my - py. Its length is the distance to the pointer, which is no good as a bullet's speed (a far click would fire a faster bullet). Normalised, it's only the direction, and times bullet_speed it's the bullet's velocity.

Hold the mouse button to fire. Each bullet is an object (Basics 7) with its own dx, dy, worked out once when it's fired, so moving the mouse afterwards doesn't bend it. cool counts down the frames between shots: 8, so 7.5 shots a second. The barrel is the same unit vector drawn 14 pixels long.

The zero-length vector turns up here as the pointer sitting exactly on the player's centre. Skipping the shot would work, but a turret with no direction looks broken, so we keep the last good aim in aim_x, aim_y, starting out pointing right. Games that aim with a stick usually do the same when the stick springs back to the middle.

mouse() returns five values: x, y, the buttons, and how far the wheel moved across and down this frame. The buttons are a bitfield: 1 for the left button and 2 for the right, added together when both are held. mb > 0 means any button; mb & 1 > 0 asks about the left one alone.

Advanced: angles

A direction can also be a single number, an angle. atan2(dx, dy) gives the angle of a vector, and cos(a), sin(a) turn an angle back into a unit vector, so aim_x, aim_y = cos(a), sin(a) points the same way. Picotron (like PICO-8) measures angles in turns: 1 is a full circle, 0.25 a quarter. It's also set up for y counting down. sin is flipped, so sin(0.25) is -1, which is up the screen: angles go round anticlockwise as you see them. atan2 matches, and answers between -0.5 and 0.5 (PICO-8's goes from 0 to 1). Its arguments are dx first, the other way round from math.atan(y, x) in Lua or Mathf.Atan2(y, x) in Unity. Picotron's cos, sin and atan2 are fast approximations, good to about 0.00005, so don't test the answer for an exact 0. Lua's own math.sin, math.cos and math.atan are there too, exact, in radians and with y up. The manual doesn't list any of these functions; this paragraph is from running them.

Advanced: more of the mouse

mouse(x, y) with arguments moves the pointer (it's in the system source rather than the manual), and the new position shows up a frame later. mouselock(true, event_sensitivity, move_sensitivity) captures the pointer and returns dx, dy, how far it moved since the last frame: another vector, for mouse-look aiming. The manual's mouselock entry has the sensitivity details.

Try this

  • The bullets start at the player's centre and fly out through the barrel. Start them at the end of the barrel instead. Which vector do you need, and how long?
  • Add spread: every shot goes off up to 0.02 turns either side of where the barrel points. (The angles note has the tools.)
  • Let the player move with slide 4's code. Should a bullet fired on the move get the player's velocity added to its own? Try both and decide which feels better.

6 Putting it together

In this lesson we learned:

  • vectors: a direction and a distance as one pair of numbers, dx, dy
  • length, sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy), which is also the distance between two points
  • normalising: divide by the length for a unit vector, then multiply by a speed
  • btn's 181, which normalises the arrow keys for us and keeps a stick's half-push
  • the vector from one thing to another, for aiming
  • the zero-length vector, and two ways round it: skip the move, or keep the last good direction

Dividing by zero doesn't stop a Picotron cart: 1 / 0 and even 0 / 0 give the largest float there is, about 1.8e308, so a position goes to inf a frame or two later and the object silently vanishes. That's why both ways round the zero-length vector matter.

The starter has slide 4's movement for the player and a red chaser that walks straight at the player at chase_speed, using the vector between them. Copy it into a new cart. Next, Techniques 2 moves things smoothly from here to there, with lerp and easing.

Try this

  • Make the chaser a homing missile that can only turn a little each frame: keep its velocity between frames, nudge it a little towards the player, then normalise it again so it stays at chase_speed. How small does the nudge have to be before you can dodge it?
  • Set chase_speed = 5, stand still and let the chaser catch you. Why does it shiver on top of you once it arrives, and what's the smallest change that stops it?
  • spr can flip a sprite but not rotate it (tline3d can, one textured line at a time), so games often draw eight, one per direction, and pick one by angle. Turn the chaser's atan2 angle into a number from 0 to 7 (0 for right, going round anticlockwise), print it and test all eight directions. Before you start: which number should "straight down" give, and what does atan2 say for it?
