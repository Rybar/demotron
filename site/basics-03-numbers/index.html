1 Doing Sums

Each row has the same sum in it twice. The grey one on the left is text we typed, and the white one is Picotron doing the maths.

The top row is these two lines:

1print("2 + 3", 170, 75, 6)
2print("= "..(2 + 3), 270, 75, 7)

In the first print we put 2 + 3 inside quotes, and Picotron never looks inside quotes, it just prints whatever's there. In the second we leave the sum outside the quotes, so Picotron adds it up to 5 first, then the two dots from Basics 2 glue that 5 onto the end of "= " and the whole lot comes out in white.

Star is times, since there's no × key. Hat is a power.

Picotron does times before minus. 10 - 4 * 2 is really 10 - 8, which is why it comes out as 2. Brackets get done first, like at school, so if we put them round the minus we get 12.

2 ^ 8 is eight 2s multiplied together, 256, except the demo prints 256.0. We'll get to that .0 on slide 3.

The whole demo:

demos/1_maths.lua
1-- text in quotes is shown as it is;
2-- the maths outside the quotes gets worked out
3function _draw()
4  cls(1)
5  print("2 + 3", 170, 75, 6)
6  print("= "..(2 + 3), 270, 75, 7)
7  print("10 - 4 * 2", 170, 105, 6)
8  print("= "..(10 - 4 * 2), 270, 105, 7)
9  print("(10 - 4) * 2", 170, 135, 6)
10  print("= "..((10 - 4) * 2), 270, 135, 7)
11  print("2 ^ 8", 170, 165, 6)
12  print("= "..(2 ^ 8), 270, 165, 7)
13end

2 Keeping Score

Copy this into Picotron and move score = 0 down into _update as its first line. When we run it the score sits at 0. It does still hit 10 once a second, for one frame, a sixtieth of a second, which is a flicker you'll barely see, because now we're setting it back to 0 sixty times a second.

Now put it back at the top where we had it. Up there it runs once, when the program starts, before the first frame. A variable is a name with a value in it, and the value stays put until some code changes it, so the score is still there next frame. Lines 2 to 4 make all three of the demo's variables.

Variable names are made of letters and digits plus the underscore, and they can't start with a digit. If we call one high-score it won't work, because Lua reads it as high minus score. Capitals matter too, Score isn't score. Lua's own words like if and end are taken.

The counting happens in the update:

1function _update()
2  timer += 1
3  if timer == 60 then
4    score += points
5    timer = 0
6  end
7end

Every update we add 1 to the timer. timer += 1 is shorthand for timer = timer + 1, and plain Lua doesn't have it, Picotron added it, along with -=, *= etc.

When the timer gets to 60 a second has gone by. The if checks for that with two equals signs, which ask a question, where one equals sign would change the timer. Then we add points to the score and start the timer again from 0. Drag points to 30 while it's running and the score doesn't jump, the next second just adds 30 where it used to add 10.

Over in _draw we clear the screen and print both numbers. The timer goes up to 59 and then shows 0, never 60, because the update that reaches 60 also resets it before the draw gets a look.

The whole demo:

demos/2_score.lua
1-- a variable remembers its value between frames
2score = 0
3points = 10
4timer = 0
5
6function _update()
7  timer += 1
8  if timer == 60 then
9    score += points
10    timer = 0
11  end
12end
13
14function _draw()
15  cls(1)
16  print("score: "..score, 200, 120, 7)
17  print("timer: "..timer, 200, 140, 6)
18end

Try This

  • Make the points go up by 5 every time they're awarded, so the score climbs faster the longer you leave it.
  • Make the score go up twice a second without touching points.

3 Two Kinds of Number

Why did 2 ^ 8 print 256.0 on the first slide?

Because it's a float. Picotron has two kinds of number, integers like 3 and floats like 3.5. A float that happens to be whole still prints its .0, and a power always gives one. We can ask math.type which kind a number is, and that's the lavender column on the right.

On line 5 we divide 7 by 2 with a slash and get 3.5. On line 7 we use a backslash, which throws the fraction away, so 7 \ 2 is 3, an integer.

Look at the text in that line though:

1print("7 \\ 2 = "..(7 \ 2), 140, 90, 7)

It has two backslashes. Inside quotes a backslash starts a special code, like the \^w\^t that made the counter double size in Basics 2, so to print one backslash we have to type two. It's a bit annoying, and it'll probably catch you out at least once.

flr is short for floor. It rounds down to an integer, so flr(3.7) is 3. Down means down, towards the minus numbers, so flr(-3.7) is -4.

A slash always gives a float, even when it divides exactly. Line 11 is 10 / 2, and it prints 5.0.

You mostly only notice any of this when you print one, like a score showing up as 50.0 because it went through a slash. We can wrap it in flr, or divide with a backslash in the first place.

PICO-8 only has one kind of number, so this one catches PICO-8 people out.

The whole demo:

demos/3_kinds.lua
1-- integers are whole numbers, floats can have
2-- a fraction; math.type tells you which
3function _draw()
4  cls(1)
5  print("7 / 2 = "..(7 / 2), 140, 60, 7)
6  print(math.type(7 / 2), 290, 60, 13)
7  print("7 \\ 2 = "..(7 \ 2), 140, 90, 7)
8  print(math.type(7 \ 2), 290, 90, 13)
9  print("flr(3.7) = "..flr(3.7), 140, 120, 7)
10  print(math.type(flr(3.7)), 290, 120, 13)
11  print("10 / 2 = "..(10 / 2), 140, 150, 7)
12  print(math.type(10 / 2), 290, 150, 13)
13  print("2 ^ 8 = "..(2 ^ 8), 140, 180, 7)
14  print(math.type(2 ^ 8), 290, 180, 13)
15end

4 Going Round

There's no if checking for the edge of the screen. It's the % 480 in the update:

1x = (x + speed) % 480

% gives the remainder after dividing. 7 % 2 is 1, because 2 goes into 7 three times with 1 left over.

Each update we add the speed to x and then take the remainder after dividing by 480, the width of the screen. Anything under 480 comes out unchanged. 480 itself comes out as 0, so with the speed on 2 we see x count up to 478 and land back on 0.

It isn't quite seamless. The circle has a radius of 12 and we only wrap its middle, so it never slides off the right edge. At 478 it's cut in half by the edge, and one frame later it's half a circle on the left with the other half off the screen. It's the simple version, and for a circle doing laps it's good enough.

The blink is % as well:

1if t() % 1 < 0.5 then
2  print("round and round", 196, 80, 7)
3end

t() counts the seconds since the program started. When we take % 1 of that the whole seconds go and only the fraction's left, and we only print the text while the fraction is under 0.5. That makes it half a second on, half a second off.

Put speed on 7.5 and x picks up a .5. It's a float now, and it stays one even if you put the speed back to 2, since a float plus an integer is a float.

The whole demo:

demos/4_wrap.lua
1-- % is the remainder: it makes things loop
2x = 0
3speed = 2
4
5function _update()
6  x = (x + speed) % 480
7end
8
9function _draw()
10  cls(1)
11  circfill(x, 135, 12, 12)
12  print("x = "..x, 8, 8, 6)
13  if t() % 1 < 0.5 then
14    print("round and round", 196, 80, 7)
15  end
16end

Try This

5 Doing It n Times

How many circles? However many n says, from none at all up to 15.

1for i = 1, n do
2  circfill(i * 30, 135, 10, i)
3  print(i, i * 30 - 2, 155, 7)
4end

for i = 1, n do runs the lines between do and end once for every number from 1 up to n, with i holding the number each time. Each time round we draw a circle i times 30 pixels across in colour i, then print i under it.

Circle 1 is colour 1, dark navy on black, so if you count one fewer than n, look harder.

With n on 0 we don't get a single circle, because the loop doesn't run at all.

A third number on the for line is the step. for i = 1, 10, 3 counts 1, 4, 7, 10.

The i belongs to the loop. Code after the end can't see it.

The whole demo:

demos/5_for.lua
1-- the loop runs its middle lines n times,
2-- with i counting 1, 2, 3 ...
3n = 5
4
5function _draw()
6  cls(0)
7  for i = 1, n do
8    circfill(i * 30, 135, 10, i)
9    print(i, i * 30 - 2, 155, 7)
10  end
11end

Try This

  • Draw the circles in a column down the screen. Fifteen of them 30 pixels apart won't fit in 270 pixels, so make them fit.
  • Make every circle bigger than the one before it.

6 Confetti

There's no cls in this _draw, on purpose.

_init runs once before the first frame and clears the screen to black, and that's the last time it gets cleared. Every draw after that we run the loop per_frame times and put down a dot each time round.

rnd(480) gives us a random number from 0 up to just under 480, fraction and all. We get a new one every time we ask, so one for x and one up to 270 for y puts the dot anywhere on the screen.

1circfill(rnd(480), rnd(270), 2, flr(rnd(16)))

The colour is flr(rnd(16)), a whole number from 0 to 15. circfill would round the colour down by itself anyway. The flr is there so you can see we want 0 to 15. Colour 0 is black, so one dot in sixteen goes down invisible.

Leave it on 30 and within half a minute there's hardly any black left. Put it on 0 and the confetti stops, and what's there stays.

rnd can pick from a list too, so rnd({8, 9, 10}) gives back one of those three. And srand(42) before the dots makes it give the same numbers every run.

The whole demo:

demos/6_confetti.lua
1-- a few random dots every frame, and the
2-- screen is only cleared once, in _init
3per_frame = 5
4
5function _init()
6  cls(0)
7end
8
9function _draw()
10  for i = 1, per_frame do
11    circfill(rnd(480), rnd(270), 2, flr(rnd(16)))
12  end
13end

Try This

  • Keep the confetti in the top half of the screen.
  • Make the confetti use only colours 8 to 10, the warm ones.
  • Wipe the screen clean every five seconds and start over.

7 Three Empty Boxes

A frame counter and three boxes with nothing in them yet. The comment in _update marks where your code goes, though anything you draw belongs in _draw.

Each update we add 1 to frames. In the draw we clear the screen, then this loop draws three outlines with rect, which takes two opposite corners:

1for i = 1, 3 do
2  rect(i * 120 - 40, 95, i * 120 + 40, 175, 13)
3end

i * 120 lands on 120, 240 and 360, which are the middles of the boxes. Each box reaches 40 pixels either side of that, from y = 95 down to 175.

None of this listens to you yet, apart from the sliders. Basics 4 reads the arrow keys, and the first thing we do there is steer a circle around.

The whole demo:

demos/7_over_to_you.lua
1-- a counter, a loop and three empty slots
2frames = 0
3
4function _update()
5  frames += 1
6  -- your ideas go here!
7end
8
9function _draw()
10  cls(1)
11  for i = 1, 3 do
12    rect(i * 120 - 40, 95, i * 120 + 40, 175, 13)
13  end
14  print("frames: "..frames, 8, 8, 7)
15end

Try This

  • In the first box, put a circle that moves left to right and comes back round without leaving the box.
  • In the second box, show how many whole seconds the program has been running, worked out from frames.
  • Fill the third box with confetti that stays inside the lines. This draw clears the screen every frame, so you'll only see this frame's dots, unless you do something about that.
