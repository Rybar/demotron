1 Picotron does maths

Under all the colours and sound, a game is mostly numbers: where things are, how fast they're moving, how many lives are left. So let's start by asking Picotron to do some sums.

Every line of the picture comes from two prints. The first prints text in quotes, and Picotron shows it exactly as written, without working anything out. The second has real maths outside the quotes, so Picotron calculates the answer first. The two dots, .., join things into one piece of text: "= "..(2 + 3) becomes = 5. The brackets keep the sum in one piece and make it easier to read.

Picotron does maths in the same order you learned at school: ^ (a power: 2 ^ 8 is eight 2s multiplied together) comes first, then * and /, then + and -. That's why 10 - 4 * 2 is 2, not 12. When you want a different order, brackets go first, just like on paper.

Don't worry about the .0 on the last answer yet. It's a clue, and we'll follow it up on slide 3.

Try this

  • Without running anything, work out 2 + 3 * 4 ^ 2. Then add a pair of lines to the demo to check your answer. Now add brackets, without changing any of the numbers, so the answer becomes 400.
  • Predict -2 ^ 2, then add it and check. If the answer surprises you, work out which happens first, the minus sign or the power, and how brackets would change it.
  • Predict what "2" .. "3" and "2" + "3" give, quotes and all, then add them to the demo and check. Is either answer a real number, or just text that looks like one? (Slide 3 shows a way to find out.)

2 Variables are named boxes

A game has to remember things from one frame to the next: the score, the number of lives, how long until the next enemy arrives. It keeps them in variables.

Think of a variable as a box with a name written on it. score = 0 makes a box called score and puts 0 inside. From then on, whenever the code says score, Picotron looks in the box and uses whatever is in there right now.

score += points means "take what's in score, add what's in points, and put the answer back in score". It's shorthand for score = score + points. A third box, timer, counts frames. _update runs 60 times a second, so when timer reaches 60, a second has passed: we add the points and empty the timer to start counting again. (if means "only when this is true". You'll meet it properly in Basics 5.)

Drag the points slider. Notice the score doesn't start again: the box keeps what it had, and each new award adds the new amount on top.

Try this

  • Move the line score = 0 to the very top of _update, just above timer += 1. Predict what the score will show, then run it. What does that tell you about where a variable that has to remember belongs?
  • Change score to scroe in the print line only, and run it. Read Picotron's error carefully: what does it say is nil (Lua's word for "nothing"), and why can't it guess which box you meant?
  • Make every award worth twice the one before: after score += points, add points *= 2 (*= works like +=, but multiplies). Predict how many seconds it takes for the score to pass a million. Then leave it running for a little over a minute, and keep watching.

3 Whole numbers and fractions

Remember the .0 from slide 1? Here's what it means. Picotron's Lua has two kinds of number. Integers are whole numbers, like 3, 0 or -12. Floats can have a fraction, like 3.5 or 0.25. math.type tells you which kind a number is, and the column on the right shows it for each answer.

/ always gives a float, even when the answer comes out whole: 10 / 2 is 5.0. That .0 is Picotron's way of saying "a float that happens to be whole". ^ is the same, so 2 ^ 8 is a float too. Most of the time you don't need to care: the two kinds mix freely, and 5.0 is still five.

When you do want a whole number, there are two tools. \ divides and rounds down: 7 \ 2 is 3, and the half is thrown away. flr (short for "floor") rounds any number down: flr(3.7) is 3. You'll reach for both all the time, for things like whole seconds or squares on a grid.

One oddity in the code: inside quotes, a backslash has a special job, so to show one we write two ("7 \\ 2 = "). Outside the quotes, 7 \ 2 is the real sum.

Try this

  • What is -7 \ 2? Predict, then add a line to check. Why does "rounds down" give that answer? (Picture the numbers on a line.)
  • Add a line for 0.1 + 0.2 - 0.3. It ought to be 0. Predict whether it will be, then check. A clue: just as 1/3 can't be written exactly as a decimal (Picotron shows 0.33333333333333), most fractions can't be stored exactly in a float.
  • Your calculator refuses to divide by zero. Predict what 7 / 0 and 7 \ 0 do in Picotron, then try them. Does the program stop? Are the two answers the same kind of number?

4 Remainders make things loop

% gives the remainder: what's left over after dividing. 7 % 3 is 1, because 3 goes into 7 twice with 1 left over. 480 % 480 is 0, and 481 % 480 is 1.

That makes % perfect for anything that goes round and round. Each frame the circle's x grows by speed, and % 480 keeps it from 0 up to (but never reaching) 480. The moment it would reach the right edge, it's back at the left. Watch the x = readout in the corner as it happens.

The blinking text uses the same idea with time. t() counts the seconds since the program started, in small steps, so t() % 1 is just the part after the point: it runs from 0 up to nearly 1, every second. For the first half of each second it's less than 0.5 and the text shows; for the second half it doesn't.

Drag speed and watch the readout. Try 2.5: floats appear, because adding a float to anything gives a float.

Try this

  • In your own copy, set speed = -3. Before you run it, predict: does the circle stop at the left edge, vanish for good, or come back on the right? Work out what -3 % 480 has to be for your answer, then check.
  • Slide 2's timer counted to 60 and was then reset by hand. Rewrite it so timer just keeps counting forever and the points still arrive once a second, using % instead of the reset.
  • Make the circle change colour four times a second, cycling through colours 8 to 15 and then back to 8. You'll want t(), % and flr: work out what each one does for you before you start typing.

5 Doing it many times: for

Drawing five circles could take five circfill lines. Fifteen circles would take fifteen lines, all nearly the same. A for loop lets you write the lines once and have Picotron repeat them.

for i = 1, n do means: make a variable i and start it at 1, run the lines down to end, then add 1 to i and run them again, until i has been every number up to n. Each time round, the circle is drawn i * 30 pixels across, so the first lands at 30, the second at 60, and so on. Its colour is i too, and the number under each circle shows what i was on that trip round the loop.

Drag the n slider. One loop, any number of circles, and the code never gets longer. At 0 the loop doesn't run at all: there's nothing to count.

Try this

  • Make a grid of circles: put a second for (counting with j) inside the first, and use j for the y position. Then make each circle's colour depend on both its row and its column. Predict the pattern i + j will make before you run it.
  • Make the radius 20 so the circles overlap. Which one ends up on top, and why? Now predict what changes if the loop counts backwards with for i = n, 1, -1 do (the third number is the step).
  • Add up every number from 1 to 100: start a variable at 0, add i to it inside a loop, and print the total. A famous mathematician is said to have found the answer in his head as a schoolboy. Once you have it, can you spot his shortcut?

6 Random numbers

Games need surprises: where the next enemy appears, which way a spark flies, what's in the chest. rnd(480) gives a random number from 0 up to (but never quite) 480, and a different one every time you ask.

rnd gives floats, like 217.38. That's fine for a position, but colours are whole numbers, so flr(rnd(16)) rounds down to one of 0, 1, 2 ... 15.

Each frame, a loop like the one on the last slide drops per_frame dots in random places and colours. And because _draw never calls cls, every dot stays where it landed, so the screen slowly fills with confetti. The cls(0) in _init clears it just once, at the start.

Drag per_frame to change how fast it fills. Each time the demo starts, the confetti lands differently: you'll never see the same screen twice.

Try this

  • Make the confetti land only in the middle third of the screen (which x values is that?), and only in the warm colours 8, 9 and 10. There are two ways to get those colours: one uses flr and +, and the other hands rnd a list in curly brackets, rnd({...}).
  • Put srand(42) inside _init, then run the cart a few times. What's different now? Why might a game want its "random" to come out the same every time?
  • Is flr(rnd(3)) fair? Use a loop to roll it 600 times, count how many 0s, 1s and 2s come up, and print the three counts. Roughly what should each one be, and does a 3 ever appear?

7 Over to you

That's the core toolkit for numbers: sums, variables that remember, whole numbers and fractions, % for things that go round, rnd for surprises and for for doing something many times.

Here's a small starting program: a frames counter that goes up by one every frame, and a loop that draws three empty slots. Copy it into a new cart in Picotron, then pick an idea below and see if you can make it work. There's no single right answer, and getting things wrong on the way is part of how everyone learns.

Try this

  • A countdown: show the seconds counting down from 10, as whole numbers, and stop at 0 instead of going negative. You'll meet max here: it takes exactly two numbers and gives back the bigger one.
  • A tiny slot machine: show a random number from 1 to 6 in each slot, changing every frame, until frames reaches 120. Then they freeze on their last numbers. (Each slot needs a variable of its own, so it remembers its number once it stops changing.)
  • Turn frames into a clock that reads minutes and seconds, like 1:05. You'll need \ and %, plus a little trick for the 0 in 1:05.
